# Running Empire Strikes Back on a Star Wars board with no slapstic

This is a way to convert an Atari Star Wars board set to play The Empire Strikes
Back **without the slapstic chip and without building an expansion board**.
The four 8K pages that the slapstic used to page go into **one 27256 EPROM**,
and two outputs of a latch **already on the main board** choose the page. No
PAL, GAL or other new chip is needed: one EPROM, two wires, and two patched ROM
images made from your own ESB ROM set.

> **Status: tested in an emulator only.** The ROM images and the paging scheme
> below have been run in this project's emulator (results at the end). Nobody
> has built this on a real board yet, and some of the board details come from
> an outside source, not from a schematic we have checked. Read
> [What is not known](#what-is-not-known) before you pick up a soldering iron.
> You do this at your own risk.

The rest of the conversion (bank jumpers, sound board, vector ROM, mathbox
PROMs) is the usual Star Wars to ESB conversion. Cliff Koch's 1996 guide, "Star
Wars to ESB Conversion Guide" (posted on GameFAQs), describes it in detail;
this note says which of its steps still apply and which do not. Credit to him
for the board-level details used here. His guide also gives a 22V10 PAL clone of
the slapstic on a hand-built daughterboard; this method replaces that whole
daughterboard.

## Why this works

Nothing in the ESB ROMs is encrypted. The slapstic 137412-101 on Atari's kit
daughterboard is a paging chip: the two expansion EPROMs (136031-105 and -106)
hold four 8K pages of ordinary 6809 code, and the slapstic decides which page
the CPU sees at `$8000-$9FFF` by watching the address bus. The game selects a
page by jumping to one of four short stubs. (The full story, including the two
tricks the game plays on the chip, is in [SLAPSTIC.md](SLAPSTIC.md).)

The patched ROMs make each stub select its page itself, by writing two bits to
the board's output latch at `$4680-$4687`. That latch already holds the ROM bank
bit W_BANK (`$4684`), and two of its outputs, LED 2 (`$4683`) and LED 3
(`$4682`), only drive diagnostic LEDs. Wired to the 27256's top two address
lines, those two outputs become the page select:

| 27256 pin | address line | driven by | page bit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 26 | A13 | output latch `$4682` (LED 3) | 0 |
| 27 | A14 | output latch `$4683` (LED 2) | 1 |

The 27256 holds page 0 at `$0000`, page 1 at `$2000`, page 2 at `$4000`,
page 3 at `$6000` (that is, 136031-105 followed by 136031-106, then patched).

## What you need

- A working Star Wars board set (main CPU, sound, AVG).
- Your own ESB ROM set (the MAME `esb` set). The ESB images are Atari's
  copyrighted material and are not supplied here.
- One 27256 or 27C256 EPROM (250 ns or faster is plenty), programmed with
  `esb_1hj.27256`.
- One 27128 (or 27C128) programmed with the patched `136031-104.1m`.
- The other ESB ROMs as they are: 136031-101, -102, -203, -111, -112, -113,
  and the mathbox PROMs 136031-107, -108, -109, -110.
- One spare 28-pin socket (machine-pin is easiest), some thin wire.

## Making the two images

From the ESB zip, with Python 3 and nothing else:

```
python tools/esb_pcb27256.py esb.zip
```

This writes `esb_1hj.27256` and `136031-104.1m` into a folder `esb_pcb27256`
next to the zip and prints their CRC32s. The tool checks the input ROMs by CRC32
and refuses any other revision. The same images come from the disassembly's own
build (`python disasm/pcb27256.py`, into `disasm/build/pcb27256/`), which also
proves the patch: see [How it was checked](#how-it-was-checked).

The no-slapstic images made by `tools/esb_unslap.py` / `disasm/unslap.py` are a
different thing. They use a page register at `$43F0` that only an emulator (or
extra decoding hardware) provides. They stay as they are; use the 27256 images
for a real board.

## Main CPU board

1. **Do not** do the daughterboard steps of Koch's guide: the cut at pin 22 of
   socket 1H/J and the jumper from pin 34 of the CPU at 2C. They exist only to
   clock the slapstic clone. If your board has already had them done for a
   daughterboard, undo them so that pin 22 of 1H/J is back on its original
   trace.

2. Do the bank jumpers exactly as in Koch's guide, section IV.A, steps 3 to 5:
   free pin 26 of the sockets at 1J/K, 1K/L and 1M from the +5 V trace it shares
   (keeping +5 V on the far side by a wire to pin 28), and jumper pin 26 of 1F,
   1J/K, 1K/L and 1M together. This gives the ROMs at `$A000-$FFFF` the bank bit,
   which ESB needs whatever is done about the slapstic.

3. Put the ESB chips in: 136031-101 at 1F, 136031-102 at 1J/K, 136031-203 at
   1K/L, the **patched** 136031-104 at 1M, and the mathbox PROMs 136031-110,
   -109, -108, -107 at 7H, 7J, 7K, 7L.

4. **Prepare the 27256 for 1H/J.** Pins 26 and 27 of the EPROM must not touch
   the board's socket. On the board they are tied to +5 V or carry something
   else, so check yours with a meter. Rather than cutting traces, plug the
   EPROM into a spare 28-pin socket with pins 26 and 27 cut short or bent out
   sideways, so that only the other 26 pins go down into 1H/J. Pin 1 (VPP) must
   still reach the board: 1H/J pin 1 is +5 V, which is what a 27256 wants
   there when reading.

5. **Find the output latch.** It is the 8-bit addressable latch decoded at
   `$4680` (MAME models it as a 74LS259) that drives W_BANK and the three
   diagnostic LEDs. One way to find it is to follow the bank signal from pin 26
   of 1F back to the latch output that drives it. On a 74LS259 the outputs
   are Q0 pin 4, Q1 pin 5, Q2 pin 6, Q3 pin 7, Q4 pin 9, Q5 pin 10, Q6 pin 11,
   Q7 pin 12. If its select inputs are A0-A2 in order, W_BANK (`$4684`) is Q4
   (pin 9), LED 3 (`$4682`) is Q2 (pin 6) and LED 2 (`$4683`) is Q3 (pin 7).
   **Confirm before wiring:** each of the two pins you pick should follow its
   LED (low = lit). At power-on the game lights all three LEDs during its
   start-up checks and turns them off when the game starts.

6. **Wire it.** A wire from the latch output for `$4682` (LED 3) to pin 26 of
   the 27256, and one from the output for `$4683` (LED 2) to pin 27. Keep them
   short. Take the signals from the latch pins themselves, not from the LED side
   of any resistor or buffer.

7. Plug the 27256 (in its adapter socket) into 1H/J, the right way round.

8. As in Koch's guide (IV.A step 8): if the board has no R59 and the game does
   not keep its high scores and settings, add the 10K resistor he describes.

## Sound board and AVG board

As in Koch's guide, sections IV.B and IV.C: on the sound board, jumper pin 26 of
1J/K and of 1H to pin 23 of 3K (after cutting their old traces), and fit
136031-112 at 1H and 136031-113 at 1J/K. On the AVG board, fit 136031-111 at 1L.

## Checking it

Check your work before powering up. Then run the self test (the test switch
on). With the images from the tool, the first screen should read **NO ERRORS
DETECTED** and **VERISUM 2915**. The unconverted ESB set reads VERISUM B735;
the emulator shows that too, the same value Koch's guide gives. A different
VERISUM means a ROM is not the one expected. An error on the expansion ROM
(error bit `$4000`) points at the 27256 or the two page wires.

The LEDs on the board stay connected and show the page bits. During play
LED 2 and LED 3 flicker as the game changes pages; that is expected. The
diagnostics still flash the LEDs as they always have. No page code runs while
they do, and the game selects its page again before it uses one.

## How it works, in detail

Each of the four page-select stubs (`$8080`, `$8090`, `$80A0`, `$80B0`, the
same 16 bytes on every page) becomes:

```
PSHS Y
LDY  #bits         ; $4682 value (page bit 0 in D7), $4683 value (page bit 1 in D7)
STY  $4682         ; writes $4682 then $4683 on consecutive cycles: the page changes here
PULS Y
BRA  $80C0         ; to an unused copy of the original BITA $8000 / RTS
```

The code is identical on all four pages, so the instruction stream carries on
correctly while the page switches underneath it, including the half-way page
between the two writes. A, B, X, U and the carry are left as the caller had
them, and the closing `BITA $8000` sets N, Z and V as the original stub did.
Each page select takes 28 more CPU cycles than with the slapstic.

Beyond the stubs, the patch changes:

- **Five page selects in the maze** where the game relied on the slapstic's two
  special modes (two "bitwise" decoys on page 3, three "alternate" selects on
  page 1). Each is pointed at the stub for the page the chip would have given.
  These are the same five places `unslap.py` changes.
- **The self test's ROM check** (bank 0 `$F0AA`, `$F0E3`, table `$EEFE`). It
  selected each page by reading a stub address, at a time when the stack
  pointer holds the error bits, so it cannot call a stub. It now loads the two
  page bits from its table and stores them with `STY $4682`, and goes back to
  page 0 with `CLR $4682 / CLR $4683`.
- **Two checksum pairs**, so that the self test's sums come out: the spare
  bytes at page 0 `$9941/$9942` and bank 0 `$FFB3/$FFB4` (in 104).

Not changed: the game's reset code already writes `$00` to all three LEDs
(page 0) and, just before the game starts, `$FF` (page 3, the slapstic's own
page at reset). So the latch's state at power-on does not matter, and its reset
behaviour does not need to be known.

## How it was checked

- `disasm/pcb27256.py` runs every call the game makes into the page-select maze
  (676 calls, from every page each can be made from) twice: once on the
  original ROMs under the slapstic model, once on the new image with the two
  latch bits, running the new stubs' actual bytes. Every call lands on the same
  routine on the same page and leaves the same page selected.
- `tools/esb_pcb27256.py` (standalone, no dependencies) gives byte-for-byte the
  same two images.
- In the emulator (`emu_src`, set `pcb27256`), the 27256's A13/A14 follow the
  latch bits and there is no slapstic model. `emu_src/tests/run_tests.py` runs
  it through the same scenarios as the original set: the attract mode (5
  minutes), two games played by a fixed pseudo-random hand (15 minutes each),
  the self test held on its results, and the self test stepped through every
  screen. All of them ran with no fault: no watchdog bite, no access to an
  unmapped address, no trap, no lost interrupt. The games ran code on all
  four pages; over the two games the latch bits were written about 790,000
  times.
- The latch's state at power-on does not matter: the attract mode ran clean
  starting from each of the four values the two bits can hold.
- The self test passes: NO ERRORS DETECTED, all six checksums zero,
  VERISUM 2915. With one bit of the 27256 flipped it reports the expansion ROM
  (error bit `$4000`) and nothing else.
- The longer stubs shift the game's timing, so a scripted game does not play
  out exactly as it does on the original set. (The same happens with the
  `unslap.py --stub write` set, whose stubs are only 5 cycles longer.) Between
  them the 27256 runs went through every wave part the original set's runs
  reached and, with the option switches at `$FA`, the asteroid field
  (phases `$27-$2C`). Two things the original set's runs happened to reach,
  these did not: initials entry for a high score (phases `$41/$42`) and the
  probot-part game over (phase `$36`).
- What the emulator cannot show: anything electrical, real EPROM timing, or
  the board's wiring.

## What is not known

- **The latch's chip location and pin-out on the real board.** The addresses
  and functions (`$4682` LED 3, `$4683` LED 2, `$4684` W_BANK) come from the
  game program and MAME. The 74LS259 type and the Q-to-address order are
  MAME's model, not a checked schematic. Confirm with a logic probe (step 5).
- **What pins 26 and 27 of 1H/J connect to on the board**, hence the adapter
  socket.
- **The LED circuit.** If an LED output is buffered, take the signal from the
  latch pin itself. An LS output driving a CMOS EPROM input as well as an LED
  should be fine, but it has not been measured.
- **Board details from Koch's 1996 guide** (socket numbers, the bank jumpers,
  the sound board jumper, 1H/J pin 1 being +5 V) are his and are taken as he
  gives them.
- **No one has run this on hardware yet.** If you do, please report what
  happened.
